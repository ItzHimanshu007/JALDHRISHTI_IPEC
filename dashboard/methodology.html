Technical note

How the flood simulation works

Jal Drishti runs a two-dimensional flood model in the browser over the whole of Meppadi panchayat (Wayanad), Darbhanga district (Bihar) and Dhemaji district (Assam). This note describes what the model computes, which inputs are real and which are made up for the demonstration, and where the results should not be trusted.

1Summary

For each area the dashboard simulates one day of steady monsoon rain: 200 mm falling at a near-constant rate from T+0 to T+24 h. Water moves over the real terrain according to the shallow-water equations, collects in valleys and low plains, and is added to by rivers entering from outside the area. Every 10–15 simulated minutes the model saves a snapshot of water depth and velocity; the map, the hexagon grid, the gauge chart and the situation figures are all read from those snapshots.

The three areas flood for different reasons, so each has its own scenario: a flash flood with a debris flow in the Western Ghats, embankment breaches on the Bihar plain, and flash tributaries against a rising Brahmaputra in Assam.

Cell sizes were chosen so that each run takes well under a minute in a browser. Meppadi's steep, narrow valleys need the finer grid; on the plains a 320–400 m cell still resolves the main rivers and embankments.

3Terrain preparation

Terrain is prepared once, offline, by scripts/build_sim_terrain.py and stored as data/sim/<area>_terrain.json. The steps are:

  1. Sample the DEM. Terrarium tiles are read at zoom 12 (Meppadi) or 11 (the plains) and resampled to the model grid.
  2. Smooth. A light Gaussian filter (σ = 0.7–1.0 cells) removes SRTM speckle, which otherwise creates false pools on flat ground.
  3. Fill pits. A priority-flood fill (Barnes et al. 2014) removes enclosed depressions that are artefacts of the DEM. Only shallow pits are filled on the plains (up to 1 m), so real low-lying basins stay; deep pits in the Dhemaji hills are filled fully.
  4. Flow accumulation. D8 flow directions (O'Callaghan & Mark 1984) give the upstream drainage area of every cell. The model uses it to find rivers entering each domain, trace their course, and set channel roughness.
  5. Boundary mask. The area boundary is rasterised onto the grid.

Slope is computed from the smoothed surface. Height above the nearest drainage line (HAND; Rennó et al. 2008) is computed in the browser by a distance transform that carries the elevation of the nearest stream cell; it drives the terrain class shown on each grid hexagon.

4Flow model

The solver (js/flood-sim-solver.js) uses the local-inertial form of the shallow-water equations, the scheme used in LISFLOOD-FP (Bates et al. 2010) with the stability treatment of de Almeida et al. (2012). Water depth h is stored at cell centres and unit discharge q (m²/s) on the faces between cells. For each face, each step:

$$q^{t+\Delta t} = \frac{q^{t} - g\,h_f\,\Delta t\;\dfrac{\partial \eta}{\partial x}}{1 + g\,\Delta t\,n^2\,|q^{t}|\,/\,h_f^{7/3}}$$

where η is the water surface elevation, hf the flow depth at the face and n Manning's roughness. Depth in each cell is then updated from the net inflow and the local sources and sinks:

$$h^{t+\Delta t} = h^{t} + \Delta t\left(\frac{\sum q_\text{in} - \sum q_\text{out}}{\Delta x} + r - f - d\right)$$

with rain r, infiltration f and urban drainage d. Three safeguards keep the explicit scheme stable on 30° slopes and 7 m-deep rivers alike:

  • the time step follows the wave-speed limit, $\Delta t = \min(30\,\text{s},\; 0.7\,\Delta x/\sqrt{g\,h_\text{max}})$;
  • discharge is capped at a Froude number of 1, $|q| \le h_f\sqrt{g\,h_f}$;
  • a cell can never lose more water in a step than it holds (positivity limiter).

Infiltration follows a Horton-type decay with the depth already infiltrated, $f = \left[f_c + (f_0 - f_c)\,e^{-F/F_k}\right]\cdot m$, where m lowers infiltration on river channels, steep thin-soiled hillsides and sealed town centres. Roughness rises from the base land value by up to 0.05 on steep hillsides (forest and plantation), falls to the channel value along rivers, and rises to 0.09 in built-up areas. A passive tracer carries sediment concentration, so debris and silt-laden river water can be told apart from ponded rain on the map.

5Rain and river inflows

Rain

Rain starts at T+0 and falls without a break for 24 hours. The rate is almost constant: a 15-minute ramp-in, then a few per cent of natural variation, scaled so the 24-hour total is exactly the storm total P (200 mm):

$$R(t) = k\left[1 + 0.04\sin\!\left(\tfrac{2\pi t}{3.7\,\text{h}}\right) + 0.025\sin\!\left(\tfrac{2\pi t}{1.3\,\text{h}} + 1\right)\right],\qquad \int_0^{24\,\text{h}} R\,dt = P$$

That is about 8 mm/h. Because nothing lets up, water keeps building and the worst state is at the end of the day. Rain is spread over each area with a weight w(x): on the Ghats and the Arunachal foothills it increases with elevation (orographic lift), and everywhere a smooth random field adds ±20 % so it is not uniform. The dashboard raises alerts when the running total passes the India Meteorological Department's 24-hour categories: heavy (64.5 mm) and very heavy (115.6 mm).

River inflows

Rivers entering a domain are found where large drainage areas cross its edge. Their flow is a triangular unit hydrograph convolved with the rain over their catchment:

$$Q(t) = Q_b + G\,\frac{\sum_\tau R(t-\tau)\,u(\tau)}{\sum_\tau u(\tau)}$$

where u rises linearly over the lag time and falls over the base time, Qb is the base flow and the gain G scales with catchment area. The Brahmaputra is not rain-driven in the model; its inflow on Dhemaji's eastern edge rises smoothly from 16,000 to 32,000 m³/s through the day.

6The three scenarios

Meppadi: flash flood and debris flow

Steep, rain-fed streams on the Western Ghats respond within an hour or two. The question is whether the valley head above Punchirimattam (the 2024 initiation zone) fails. Slope stability uses the infinite-slope factor of safety (Skempton & DeLory 1957), with the soil's saturation m rising with the rain that has fallen on the slope:

$$FS = \frac{c' + (\gamma - m\,\gamma_w)\,z\cos^2\!\beta\,\tan\phi'}{\gamma\,z\,\sin\beta\cos\beta},\qquad m = \min\!\left(1,\;\frac{w\,P_\text{cum}}{170\ \text{mm}}\right)$$

with cohesion c' = 9 kPa, friction angle φ' = 34°, soil depth z = 2.5 m, soil unit weight γ = 19 kN/m³ and γw = 9.81 kN/m³. When FS drops below 1 on the steepest cell of the source area, a debris pulse (about 1.9 million m³ at 200 mm) is released over 15 minutes and routed by the flow model down the Punnapuzha through Mundakkai and Chooralmala. In the current storm this happens at T+13.1 h. The map's hatched slopes are cells steeper than 26° that fail when saturated.

Darbhanga: embankment breaches and drainage congestion

Floods here come down the embanked rivers from the Nepal side. The four largest rivers entering on the north and west edges are traced downstream. Each gets a channel carved 2 m into the plain, starts 2 m deep, and is lined on both banks by embankments 3.6 m high. Their catchments extend well beyond the domain (taken as six times the drainage area at the edge) and respond with a lag of 3–4 h.

Every river crossing the district has one breach site, on its weakest (lowest) bank; on the river nearest Darbhanga town it is the weakest bank just upstream of the town. The solver watches the water level there. After an hour above Danger Level (1.3 m above bank level, or, at the town site, 1.2 m above the river's own level at T+0), the embankment cells are lowered and the flow model carries water through the gap. With 200 mm of rain the three embankments fail at T+5.6, T+8.6 and T+12.7 h. At T+6 h the town outfall sluices close because the rivers are above them, and town drainage falls to 20 % of capacity; rain trapped between embankments then ponds across the plain.

Dhemaji: flash tributaries and backwater

Six north-bank rivers (the Jiadhal and its neighbours) come off the Arunachal foothills with short lags (1.8–2.6 h) and carry sand and silt. On a 400 m grid the gorges above the foothills cannot be resolved, so each river is injected where it reaches the plain. Meanwhile the rising Brahmaputra on the southern and eastern side drowns their outfalls, so water spreads as wide, shallow sheets rather than draining away.

7From water depth to risk

Floodwater is water above the level at T+0. Rivers already flowing in their banks and permanent water bodies are not flooding, so every figure on the dashboard (inundated area, water accumulated, people in water, grid colours) uses the depth above that starting level. That is why every grid cell is green before the simulation runs.

Thresholds used for people

At risk to lifedepth > 1.5 m, or depth × speed > 1 m²/sadults lose footing in fast flow (Jonkman & Penning-Rowsell 2008)

Residents per cell come from the dashboard's density model: district census density, concentrated around named settlements. River-channel cells keep a quarter of that density, since people live on banks, embankments and river islands but not in the channel itself.

Risk grid

Each area is divided into about 220 hexagons of equal size (IDs like DBG-F12, row A northernmost). Before the run, a hexagon shows its residents and terrain: elevation range, mean slope, height above the nearest river, distance to that river and a terrain class. As the simulation runs, each hexagon is classed by how much of it is under water and how many of its residents are in water. The classes are relative to the hexagon, so a large, sparsely populated hexagon is not rated severe for a small pond.

In Darbhanga, where the rivers themselves are the main hazard, a hexagon that a river runs through is also rated by how far that river has risen: at least moderate at 0.4 m, high at 1 m and severe at 2 m above its T+0 level.

8What the map shows

The water surface is drawn from the model's depth field, blended between the two snapshots either side of the clock time, and draped over the 3D terrain. Like the grid and the figures, it shows floodwater: water above the level at T+0, from 0.10 m deep. River channels are drawn at full depth, so before the storm only the rivers show. Colour follows depth (thin sheets are pale and translucent, deep water darker) and turns brown where the sediment tracer is high. Thin runoff sheets on steep hillsides are hidden, because they are not flooding. Arrows show the depth-weighted mean flow of each block of cells, larger and brighter where the water moves faster; more of them appear as you zoom in. A faint silt stain marks ground that was flooded and has since drained. The gauge chart reads the same snapshots at one river cell, so it always agrees with the map.

9Checks and run times

Mass balance. At the end of every run the solver reports rain, inflow, infiltration, drainage, boundary outflow and stored water. The balance closes to better than 0.001 % in all three areas.

10Limitations

  • The forcing is synthetic. Rain, river inflows and failure timings are plausible but invented. The results show how each area responds to a storm of this kind, not a forecast of a real event.
  • Not yet validated. The model has not been compared with observed flood extents, so its accuracy is unknown. It should not be used for real decisions until it has been.
  • SRTM is coarse on flat ground. Its vertical error (several metres) is comparable to the relief of the Bihar plain. Embankments and road levels are not in the DEM; the modelled embankments are idealised.
  • Grid resolution. At 320–400 m, streets, buildings, culverts and small channels are not resolved. Town drainage is a uniform sink, not a pipe network.
  • Population is modelled. Residents per cell are an estimate from census density, not a count. Exposure figures are indicative.
  • Risk classes are rules, not a trained model. The thresholds are set by judgement and have not been calibrated against damage or casualty data.
