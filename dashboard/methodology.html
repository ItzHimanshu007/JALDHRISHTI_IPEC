Technical note · version 0.9 · 27 September 2026

Simulating monsoon floods in Meppadi, Darbhanga and Dhemaji

Team Jal Drishti

Summary. Jal Drishti is a flood operations dashboard for three very different places: a hill panchayat in the Western Ghats, an embanked river plain in north Bihar and a Brahmaputra floodplain district in Assam. Behind the map is a two-dimensional shallow-water model that runs in the browser over each whole area on real SRTM terrain. It simulates one day of continuous monsoon rain (400 mm in Meppadi, 200 mm on the plains), with the way each place actually floods built in: slope failure and debris flow, embankment breaches, and flash tributaries against a high main river. The water it produces drives everything else on the dashboard: the risk grid, the exposure figures, the gauge chart and the evacuation routes. The storms are synthetic; the terrain is not. This note describes the model, the choices we made, what the results look like and where they should not be trusted.

1. Background

The three areas were picked because each floods in a different way, and a single generic "flood risk" layer would describe none of them well.

Meppadi, Wayanad (Kerala). On 30 July 2024, after two days of extreme rain, a debris flow that started on the slopes above Punchirimattam swept down the Punnapuzha valley through Mundakkai and Chooralmala. More than 200 people were killed. The hazard here is not standing water but fast, sediment-laden flow in narrow valleys, triggered when saturated hillsides fail.

Darbhanga (Bihar). The district lies across the Kamla–Balan and Bagmati river systems that come down from Nepal. The rivers are confined by long earthen embankments, and when one breaches the water spreads across a plain that is almost flat. In August 2008 the Kosi, to the east, broke its embankment at Kusaha and flooded large parts of north Bihar, affecting more than three million people. Once the rivers run high, sluices on the town outfalls are shut and rain has nowhere to drain, so waterlogging adds to the river flood.

Dhemaji (Assam). Short, steep rivers such as the Jiadhal come off the Arunachal foothills and rise within hours of heavy rain, carrying sand that buries fields and shifts their channels. When the Brahmaputra is high at the same time, their outfalls are drowned and the water spreads out as wide, shallow sheets across the district.

4. Terrain

Terrain is prepared by scripts/build_sim_terrain.py and stored as data/sim/<area>_terrain.json.

  1. Sample. Terrain tiles are read at zoom 12 for Meppadi and 11 for the plains and resampled to the model grid.
  2. Smooth. A light Gaussian filter (σ = 0.7–1.0 cells) removes SRTM speckle, which otherwise creates false pools on flat ground.
  3. Fill pits. A priority-flood fill (Barnes et al. 2014) removes enclosed hollows that are artefacts of the DEM. On the plains only pits up to 1 m deep are filled, so real low-lying basins stay; deep artefact pits in the Dhemaji hills are filled completely.
  4. Flow accumulation. D8 flow directions (O'Callaghan and Mark 1984) give the upstream area of every cell. The scenarios use it to find rivers entering each domain, trace their course and set channel roughness.
  5. Boundary. The area boundary is rasterised onto the grid.

Height above the nearest drainage line (HAND; Rennó et al. 2008) is computed in the browser and used, with slope, to describe each hexagon of the risk grid before the storm.

5. Flow model

The solver (js/flood-sim-solver.js) uses the local-inertial form of the shallow-water equations used in LISFLOOD-FP (Bates et al. 2010), with the stability treatment of de Almeida et al. (2012). Depth h is held at cell centres and discharge per unit width q on the faces between cells. For each face and time step:

$$q^{t+\Delta t} = \frac{q^{t} - g\,h_f\,\Delta t\,\dfrac{\partial \eta}{\partial x}}{1 + g\,\Delta t\,n^2\,|q^{t}|\,/\,h_f^{7/3}}$$

where η is the water surface, hf the flow depth at the face and n Manning's roughness. Depth is then updated from the net flow and the local sources and sinks, rain r, infiltration f and town drainage d:

$$h^{t+\Delta t} = h^{t} + \Delta t\left(\frac{\sum q_\text{in} - \sum q_\text{out}}{\Delta x} + r - f - d\right)$$

The scheme is explicit, so three things keep it stable on 30° slopes and in 7 m deep rivers alike: the time step follows the wave-speed limit, $\Delta t = \min(30\,\text{s},\ 0.7\,\Delta x/\sqrt{g\,h_\text{max}})$; discharge is capped at a Froude number of 1; and no cell can lose more water in a step than it holds.

Infiltration decays with the depth already absorbed, $f = \left[f_c + (f_0 - f_c)\,e^{-F/F_k}\right] m$ (a Horton-type curve; Horton 1933), where m reduces it on river beds, on steep thin-soiled slopes and in built-up centres. Roughness increases by up to 0.05 on steep forested or plantation slopes and is 0.09 in built-up areas. A passive tracer carries sediment concentration, which is how debris and silty river water are told apart from ponded rain on the map.

6. Storms and rivers

Rain starts at T+0 and falls without a break for 24 hours at a near-constant rate: a 15-minute ramp-in, then a few per cent of variation, scaled so the day's total is exactly P:

$$R(t) = k\left[1 + 0.04\sin\!\left(\tfrac{2\pi t}{3.7\,\text{h}}\right) + 0.025\sin\!\left(\tfrac{2\pi t}{1.3\,\text{h}} + 1\right)\right],\qquad \int_0^{24\,\text{h}} R\,dt = P$$

P is 400 mm for Meppadi (about 17 mm/h) and 200 mm for Darbhanga and Dhemaji (about 8 mm/h). The Western Ghats receive far heavier rain than the plains; 400 mm in a day is still less than Wayanad received over the two days before the 2024 landslides. Because the rain never lets up, the worst state is at the end of the day. Within each area the rain is weighted towards high ground on the Ghats and the Arunachal foothills, with a smooth random variation of ±20 %. Alerts follow the India Meteorological Department's daily categories: heavy (64.5 mm), very heavy (115.6 mm) and extremely heavy (204.5 mm).

Rivers entering a domain are found where large drainage areas cross its edge. Their flow is a triangular unit hydrograph convolved with the catchment rain,

$$Q(t) = Q_b + G\,\frac{\sum_\tau R(t-\tau)\,u(\tau)}{\sum_\tau u(\tau)}$$

where u rises over the lag time and falls over the base time, Qb is base flow and the gain G scales with catchment area. The Brahmaputra is not rain-driven in the model: its inflow on Dhemaji's eastern edge rises from 16,000 to 32,000 m³/s during the day.

7. Scenarios

Meppadi: slope failure and debris flow

Stability of the valley head above Punchirimattam is judged with the infinite-slope factor of safety (Skempton and DeLory 1957). Soil saturation m rises with the rain that has fallen on the slope:

$$FS = \frac{c' + (\gamma - m\,\gamma_w)\,z\cos^2\!\beta\,\tan\phi'}{\gamma\,z\,\sin\beta\cos\beta},\qquad m = \min\!\left(1,\ \frac{w\,P_\text{cum}}{170\ \text{mm}}\right)$$

with cohesion c' = 9 kPa, friction angle φ' = 34°, soil depth z = 2.5 m and unit weights γ = 19 and γw = 9.81 kN/m³. When FS drops below 1 on the steepest cell of the source area, 2.6 million m³ of debris is released over 15 minutes and the flow model routes it down the Punnapuzha through Mundakkai and Chooralmala. With 400 mm this happens at T+6.8 h, and a smaller failure on the adjoining spur follows at T+7.6 h. Slopes steeper than 26° that fail when saturated are hatched on the map.

Darbhanga: embankment breaches and drainage congestion

The four largest rivers entering on the north and west edges are traced downstream. Each is given a channel 2 m below the plain, 2 m of water at the start and embankments 3.6 m high on both banks. Their catchments reach far into Nepal (taken as six times the drainage area at the edge) and respond with a lag of 3–4 h.

Each river crossing the district has one breach site on its weakest bank; on the river nearest Darbhanga town it is the weakest bank just upstream of the town. The solver watches the water level there, and after an hour above Danger Level (1.3 m above bank level, or 1.2 m above the river's own T+0 level at the town site) the embankment is lowered and water flows through the gap. The three embankments fail at T+5.6, T+8.6 and T+12.7 h. At T+6 h the town outfall sluices close and town drainage drops to a fifth, so rain trapped between embankments ponds across the plain.

Dhemaji: flash tributaries and backwater

Six north-bank rivers, the Jiadhal among them, come off the foothills with lags of 1.8–2.6 h and carry sand and silt. A 400 m grid cannot resolve the gorges above the foothills, so each river enters the model where it reaches the plain. The rising Brahmaputra holds their outfalls up, and the water spreads as broad, shallow sheets.

8. From depth to risk

Floodwater is counted as water above the level at T+0, so rivers flowing in their banks are not flooding. Every figure on the dashboard, and the water drawn on the map, uses this depth.

At risk to lifedepth over 1.5 m, or depth × speed over 1 m²/sadults lose their footing in fast water (Jonkman and Penning-Rowsell 2008)

Each area is divided into about 220 equal hexagons (IDs such as DBG-F12, row A in the north). Each hexagon is classed by the share of its land under water and the share of its residents in water, so a large, thinly populated hexagon is not rated severe for one pond.

In Darbhanga a hexagon that a river runs through is also rated by how far the river has risen: at least moderate at 0.4 m, high at 1 m and severe at 2 m.

9. Evacuation routes

The evacuation plan (js/flood-routes.js) routes every hexagon rated high or severe at the current model time. Each one starts from the 100 m to 400 m cell inside it where most residents are standing in water, preferring cells they can still wade out of, and ends at the nearest safe place. Clicking anywhere else on the map plans up to three alternatives from that point. Routes only ever end at a safe place: a listed shelter, hospital, high-ground point or rescue base that stays dry in the model to T+24 h, or raised ground found by scanning the area in 1 km (hills) or 3 km (plains) blocks for the highest gentle cell that never floods. On the Bihar plain this picks out the embankment crests, where people do shelter in real floods. Listed places that the model floods are left out, and the route panel says so.

A single Dijkstra search (Dijkstra 1959) on the model grid gives the travel time to every candidate. Walking speed follows Tobler's hiking function (Tobler 1993) for slope and slows in shallow water. Water deeper than 0.45 m or fast water (depth × speed over 0.5 m²/s) expected in the next two hours cannot be walked through; crossing a river on foot costs the time to find a bridge. Walking is slowed to 60 % of normal speed in high-risk cells and 40 % in severe ones, so a route leaves the worst ground by the shortest way and goes round other danger cells when a detour costs little. For the plan, the search runs once backwards from all safe places together, which gives every cell its quickest way out in a fraction of a second; the plan is recomputed whenever the timeline stops. If the start is already in deep water, or every way out on foot is cut, the route is planned for a rescue boat at 6 km/h through deep slack water, then on foot. Cells surrounded by fast water that a boat cannot work in are marked cut off, for airlift or sheltering in place. Paths are straightened where the straight line is no worse and rounded by corner cutting (Chaikin 1974).

The three areas behave as their flood types suggest. Meppadi floods within the first seven hours and then levels off: its valleys are steep, so extra rain runs out of the panchayat about as fast as it falls, and the debris flow shows as a spike at T+7 h. Darbhanga starts slowly and accelerates as each breach opens and the sluices close. Dhemaji rises steadily as the tributaries and the Brahmaputra keep coming up.

Checks. Every run reports rain, inflow, infiltration, drainage, boundary outflow and stored water; the balance closes to better than 0.001 % in all three areas. Run times are for one CPU thread in Node.js; in the browser the run happens in a Web Worker while playback starts.

11. Limitations

  • Synthetic forcing. The storms, river inflows and failure times are plausible but made up. The results show how each area responds to a storm of this kind; they are not a forecast.
  • Not validated. The model has not yet been compared with observed flood extents, so its accuracy is unknown, and it should not be used for real decisions until it has been.
  • Terrain. SRTM's vertical error of several metres is comparable to the relief of the Bihar plain. Real embankments and road levels are not in it; the modelled embankments are idealised.
  • Resolution. At 320–400 m, streets, culverts and small drains are not resolved, and town drainage is a uniform sink rather than a pipe network.
  • People. Residents per cell are estimated from census density, not counted.
  • Risk classes. The hexagon rules are set by judgement and have not been calibrated against damage or casualty records.
